Who This Is For

Recommended for primary liver cancers such as hepatocellular carcinoma, selected secondary (metastatic) liver cancers including those spreading from bowel cancer, intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma, neuroendocrine tumour metastases, and other localised liver conditions where removing the affected portion is the right treatment.

What It Involves

Surgical removal of part of the liver, performed using an open or robotic-assisted approach depending on what's appropriate for your case. In robotic surgery, several small incisions replace a single large one, a camera and instruments connect to a robotic system, and Mr Iype operates from a console, controlling every instrument movement throughout, the robot does not operate independently. The approach gives a magnified three-dimensional view and precise instrument control, useful during complex resections involving nearby blood vessels. Occasionally, a case started robotically needs to continue through a larger open incision, a safety decision rather than a sign anything has gone wrong.

Benefits of This Approach

The liver's capacity to regenerate allows a significant portion to be safely removed, with the remaining liver usually increasing in size and function over the following weeks and months. Where robotic surgery is appropriate, patients may see smaller scars, less postoperative pain, reduced blood loss, earlier mobilisation and a faster return to normal activity than with open surgery. The aim throughout is the same regardless of approach: remove the cancer safely while preserving as much healthy liver as possible.

Risks and Considerations

As with any liver surgery, there are risks specific to the operation, which Mr Iype will discuss with you in full before you decide to proceed. These include:

  • Bleeding, given the liver's rich blood supply, occasionally requiring a blood transfusion or further procedure
  • Bile leak from the cut surface of the liver, most settle without further surgery
  • A fluid collection or infection around the liver, sometimes needing antibiotics or drainage
  • Liver failure, if the remaining liver doesn't function adequately, uncommon but serious
  • Rarely, injury to nearby structures such as the bowel, bile ducts, or blood vessels

Your individual risk depends on the extent of surgery, the condition of your liver, and your general health, and will be discussed with you individually.

Preparation

Imaging (CT and/or MRI) to assess tumour position and how much healthy liver tissue will remain, blood tests, and a pre-assessment appointment. Your case is usually reviewed by a specialist multidisciplinary team beforehand. If you take blood-thinning medicines, you'll be given individual instructions on what to continue or stop.

Recovery & Hospital Stay

Generally a hospital stay of a few days to two weeks depending on the extent of surgery, with early mobilisation and a gradual return to eating encouraged from the first day. Fatigue is normal in the weeks after and improves gradually; most patients are advised to build up activity progressively and avoid heavy lifting until cleared.
